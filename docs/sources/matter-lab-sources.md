Florida benchmark on p.231: **SC.5.P.8.1** Compare and contrast the basic properties of solids, liquids, and

## p.231 — Lesson opener: "What Are Solids, Liquids, and Gases?"
Engage question: bottled water and the snow from a snow machine are both water. How are these forms different?
(Answer idea: same type of matter, different state — liquid water takes the bottle's shape; snow is solid ice
crystals with their own shape; the state changed because energy/temperature changed.)
Reading skill: compare and contrast (how are things alike, how are they different).
Lesson vocabulary (6 terms): matter, volume, temperature, solid, liquid, gas.

## p.232 — "What's the Matter?"
- Air is matter because it has mass and it has volume, even though you can't see it or grab it.
- Matter cannot be created or destroyed. It might change form, but it is still matter.
- Mass = the amount of matter in something. Every tiny particle of matter has mass.
- Volume = the amount of space something takes up. Blowing air into a balloon shows air has volume.
- Caption: the large pencil has more matter than the smaller pencils — more mass and more volume.
- Activity: in a craft picture, what matter is soft and sticky (glue/clay), what is hard and sharp (scissors).

## p.233 — "Matter Has Properties"
- Properties are characteristics used to describe or identify matter (apple juice: gold color, sweet taste,
  pours easily). All matter has properties.
- Physical properties can be observed WITHOUT changing the matter into a new substance. Examples on the page:
  odor (lilac, onion), texture (sandpaper is rough; a glass vase is smooth), color (paint palette).
- Texture is how something feels. Observing that sandpaper is rough does not change the sandpaper.
- Activity: Venn diagram comparing two stones (a red gem and a gold-colored rock).

## p.234 — "More Properties"
- Color, texture, and odor are just a few physical properties.
- Temperature is a measure of the energy of motion of the particles in matter. Melted glass has a very high
  temperature. Temperature can be measured with a thermometer.
- Volume example: the food in the small dog's bowl has less volume than the food in the big dog's bowl
  because it takes up less space. Many tools can measure volume.
- Active reading: everyday words with a different meaning in science (e.g., "matter", "volume", "mass").
- Edge of page shows the start of a math/density table with gelatin, pudding, whipped cream (continued on p.235,
  which was not supplied).

## p.236 — "States of Matter"
- State is another physical property of matter. Solid, liquid, and gas are the most common states on Earth.
- Liquid: has a definite volume but NOT a definite shape. Particles move slower than gas particles and slide
  by each other.
- Gas: no definite shape and no definite volume. Particles move very quickly and are far apart.

## p.237
- Matter is made of tiny particles. Particles in solids, liquids, and gases have different amounts of energy.
  The amount of energy affects how fast particles move and how close together they are.
- Gas particles are affected little by other particles, so they move throughout their container. Gases take
  both the shape AND the volume of their container.
- Liquid particles can't move as freely; a liquid keeps the same volume in any container, but because the
  particles slide by each other, it takes the shape of its container.
- Solid particles do not move from place to place, so solids keep the same shape and volume.
- Solid: definite shape and volume; particles very close together; they don't move from place to place, they
  just vibrate where they are (photo: metal horse statue).
- Fish tank activity: bubbles = gas, water = liquid, castle = solid.

## p.238 — "A Matter of Temperature"
- An ice cube melting on a hot day is a change caused by a change in temperature. When matter changes state,
  the TYPE of matter is not changed (ice, water, and water vapor are all water).
- When matter takes in or releases energy, its temperature changes. When enough energy is taken in or released,
  matter can change state.
- Gas releases energy → temperature goes down until it condenses (changes to a liquid).
- Liquid releases energy → temperature goes down until it freezes (changes to a solid).
- Solid takes in energy → temperature rises until it melts (changes to a liquid).
- Liquid takes in energy → temperature rises until it evaporates (changes to a gas).
- Evaporation and boiling both turn liquids into gases. Evaporation is slower and happens only at a liquid's
  surface. Boiling is faster and happens throughout the liquid.
- Photos: ice in a bowl heated → water → boiling. "When a liquid absorbs enough energy, the liquid boils, or
  rapidly changes to a gas."

## p.239
- Gas releases enough energy → condenses to liquid: water vapor condenses into raindrops and dew.
- Liquid releases enough energy → freezes: dripping water that freezes can form icicles.
- The temperature at which a type of matter freezes or melts is the same. The temperature at which it condenses
  or boils is also the same. For water: melting/freezing point 0 °C; condensation/boiling point 100 °C.
  (Say "at sea level / normal air pressure" if you mention 100 °C as a fact; the PDF says "~100 °C (212 °F)".)
- Evaporation can happen at temperatures below the boiling point (a puddle dries on a warm day).
- Lava is hot, melted rock; it releases energy as it cools and becomes solid rock.
- Organizer: as a solid takes in energy its temperature rises; eventually it melts, changing to a liquid. If the
  liquid takes in enough energy it will boil/evaporate, changing to a gas.

## p.240 — "Properties of Solids, Liquids, and Gases"
- Each material has its own properties, but properties can change depending on the state of the material.
- Each state has different physical properties. Liquids and gases both flow (move from place to place).
  Gases can expand (take up more space) or compress (take up less space). Solids have definite textures.
- Liquids: all liquids flow; different liquids may flow at different rates — liquid water flows much more
  quickly than honey.

## p.241
- Solids: clay and a wooden table are both solids but feel different. All solids have a shape, but the shape of
  some solids (clay) can be changed easily.
- Gases: a lot of gas is compressed in a tank under high pressure; compressed gas from the tank expands to fill
  many balloons.
- Main-idea organizer: Main idea = each state of matter has different physical properties. Liquids — motor oil
  and milk FLOW at different rates. Gases — when you push on the sides of a balloon, the gas inside is
  COMPRESSED. Solids — glass and sandpaper have different TEXTURES.

## Family review PDF ("Science Made Easy", 5 pages) — key content
- Matter = anything that has mass and takes up space. Even invisible air is matter. Memory line:
  "Mass = amount · Volume = space · Density = packed!"
- Solid = STAYS PUT (own shape and volume; tightly packed particles vibrate in place; ice, rock, desk).
  Liquid = FLOWS (keeps volume, takes container's shape; particles slide past each other; water, milk, honey).
  Gas = SPREADS OUT / FILLS (no fixed shape or volume; particles move quickly and spread out to fill the
  container; air, helium, water vapor). Trick: SOLID = STAYS · LIQUID = FLOWS · GAS = FILLS.
- Physical properties = clues that describe matter without changing it into a new substance: color, texture,
  odor, temperature (linked to particle motion), mass, volume, density (mass ÷ volume).
  "A basketball and a bowling ball can be about the same size, but the bowling ball has more mass."
- Density = mass ÷ volume. Table (each sample 100 cm³): gelatin 75 g → 0.75 g/cm³; pudding 90 g → 0.90 g/cm³;
  whipped cream 50 g → 0.50 g/cm³. Pudding is most dense: most mass in the same space.
- Gaining heat energy: solid → (melting) → liquid → (evaporation/boiling) → gas.
  Losing heat energy: gas → (condensation) → liquid → (freezing) → solid.
  Melting: solid → liquid (ice → water). Freezing: liquid → solid. Evaporation: liquid → gas slowly at the
  surface. Boiling: liquid → gas rapidly throughout the liquid. Condensation: gas → liquid (vapor → droplets).
  Water freezes/ice melts at 0 °C (32 °F); water boils at about 100 °C (212 °F).
- Three sneaky test facts: gases can be squeezed (compression); liquids flow at different speeds (water faster
  than honey); some solids are soft (clay changes shape but stays a solid).
- The PDF's own 5-question quiz (do NOT copy it verbatim — make fresh variations): sunlight is not matter
  (energy); a liquid has fixed volume but no fixed shape; water vapor cooling into droplets = condensation;
  80 g ÷ 100 cm³ = 0.8 g/cm³; a balloon can be squeezed because gas can be compressed.
